# Part C, Task 4 — Halving ResetCurrent to 100 µA

## What happened to area: nothing

| | Area | Read latency | Write latency | Write energy | Leakage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline (Reset 200 µA) | 3.008 mm² | 1.727 ns | 10.871 ns | 497 pJ | 265.301 mW |
| **ResetCurrent 100 µA** | **3.008 mm²** | **1.727 ns** | **10.871 ns** | **497 pJ** | **265.301 mW** |
| Reset *and* Set 100 µA | 3.008 mm² | 1.698 ns | 10.843 ns | 399 pJ | 265.202 mW |

Reported cell area stays at `54.000 (10.392Fx5.196F)` in all three.

Halving `-ResetCurrent` alone changes **literally nothing** — not one digit of any
output. Two separate reasons:

1. `SubArray.cpp:750` takes `writeDynamicEnergy = MAX(cellResetEnergy,
   cellSetEnergy)`, and `-SetCurrent` is still 200 µA, so the max is unchanged.
   Halving both does drop write energy 497 → 399 pJ.
2. **Area does not depend on write current at all**, in either direction.

## The mechanism the tool does not model

The assignment's note states the chain: *write current → sizes the access
transistor → sets cell area → sets the density advantage you bought the
technology for.* Every link of that chain is broken in NVSim.

**Link 1 — current does not size the transistor.** `-AccessCMOSWidth (F): 6` is
read straight from the cell file (`MemCell.cpp:326`). NVSim never checks whether
a 6 F device can actually deliver 200 µA. The width is used only for on-resistance
and gate/drain capacitance (`SubArray.cpp:250-252`), never for sizing.

**Link 2 — transistor width does not set cell area.** `MemCell.cpp:141`:

```c
heightInFeatureSize = sqrt(area * aspectRatio);
widthInFeatureSize  = sqrt(area / aspectRatio);
```

Cell area is a pure input. `widthAccessCMOS` appears nowhere in it.

Write current *is* used — `SubArray.cpp:123` computes `maxBitlineCurrent` for
wordline and bitline IR/electromigration limits — but that is a wire-sizing check,
not a cell-area calculation. So the single most important coupling in NVM array
design is, in this tool, not a coupling at all.

## Doing the chain by hand

**Step 1: is 6 F consistent with 200 µA?** NVSim's own 45 nm HP device delivers
`currentOnNmos ≈ 1707 µA/µm` at 350 K. A 6 F = 0.27 µm device gives 461 µA
nominal, so the cell file implicitly assumes the access transistor delivers about
**43 % of its nominal drive**. That is the right order: the MTJ sits in series, so
at 200 µA through R_on = 3 kΩ it drops 0.6 V, leaving V_ds = 0.4 V; and in the
direction where the MTJ is on the source side the source rises to 0.6 V, cutting
V_gs to 0.4 V against V_th = 0.127 V plus body effect. The access device is
source-degenerated by the very element it is writing. 6 F for 200 µA is
self-consistent.

**Step 2: width scales with current.** At fixed overdrive, drive current is
linear in width, so 100 µA needs ≈ 3 F and 400 µA needs ≈ 12 F.

**Step 3: width sets cell area.** The 54 F² cell is 10.392 F × 5.196 F. Taking
the long dimension as transistor width plus contacts and isolation, 6 F of
transistor leaves 4.392 F of overhead. Holding that overhead and the 5.196 F
bitline-direction pitch fixed:

| Write current | Access width | Cell area | vs 6T SRAM (146 F²) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 µA | 3 F | **38.4 F²** | 3.80× denser |
| 200 µA | 6 F | 54.0 F² | 2.70× denser |
| 300 µA | 9 F | 69.6 F² | 2.10× denser |
| 400 µA | 12 F | 85.2 F² | 1.71× denser |

**Step 4: feed that back into NVSim.** Re-running with the cell area and access
width made consistent with each write current — `iw/cell_*.cell` — gives what the
tool should have produced on its own:

| Write current | Array area | vs SRAM | Read latency | Write latency | Write energy | Leakage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 µA | **2.290 mm²** | 5.01× | 1.499 ns | 10.635 ns | 358 pJ | 243.9 mW |
| 200 µA | 3.008 mm² | 3.81× | 1.727 ns | 10.871 ns | 497 pJ | 265.3 mW |
| 300 µA | 3.580 mm² | 3.21× | 1.942 ns | 11.006 ns | 625 pJ | 281.9 mW |
| 400 µA | 4.151 mm² | 2.76× | 2.171 ns | 11.140 ns | 750 pJ | 286.6 mW |

**Halving the write current shrinks the array 24 %, 3.008 → 2.290 mm²**, and
improves read latency 13 %, write energy 28 %, and leakage 8 % along with it.
Doubling it to 400 µA inflates the array 38 % and erodes the density advantage
over SRAM from 3.81× to 2.76×.

## Why this is the coupling that matters

Everything STT-MRAM is sold on runs through the write current.

**Density is a write-current claim in disguise.** The 54 F² headline is not a
property of the MTJ — the MTJ is in the back-end metal and occupies no silicon
footprint. 54 F² is the area of *the transistor needed to write it*. Quote a cell
area without quoting the write current it assumes and the number means nothing.

**The area penalty is worse than linear in the array.** A bigger cell lengthens
every wordline and bitline, so read latency degrades (1.499 → 2.171 ns across the
sweep, 45 %) even though nothing about the read path changed. Cell area is the
input to the whole array model; perturbing it moves everything.

**And the write current cannot simply be lowered.** I_c0 is set by the same
energy barrier E_b that gives retention, the same barrier Task 3's argument turned
on. Lower the current and the cell either switches more slowly — the 10 ns pulse
grows — or retains for less time. So the real design space is a three-way bind:

```
retention (E_b)  ──  write current I_c  ──  cell area
       │                    │                   │
       └──── write time ────┘              density claim
```

You cannot improve any one corner without paying at another. A device paper that
reports a low switching current alongside a long retention time is claiming to
have moved the whole triangle — usually through materials work such as perpendicular
anisotropy or interface engineering — and *that* is the claim worth checking,
far more than the TMR headline of Task 3.

**For Part E.** The STT-MRAM case rests on 3.81× density and 8.5× lower leakage.
Both are contingent on 200 µA. If the real device needs 400 µA, density falls to
2.76× and the array grows 38 %; if materials work reaches 100 µA, density rises to
5.01×. None of that sensitivity is visible from NVSim's outputs, because the tool
treats cell area as an independent input rather than a consequence. The sensitivity
has to be carried by hand.
